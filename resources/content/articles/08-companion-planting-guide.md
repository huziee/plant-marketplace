# Companion Planting Guide: What Grows Well Together

**Meta Description:** Discover the best companion planting combinations for your garden, from classic pairings to plants you should always keep apart.

**Target Keyword:** companion planting guide
**Secondary Keywords:** what plants grow well together, companion planting chart, vegetable companion planting

---

Companion planting is the practice of growing certain plants near each other for mutual benefit — better pest control, improved growth, more efficient use of space, or even better flavor. It's a technique gardeners have relied on for generations, and while not every claimed benefit is scientifically proven, many pairings have solid evidence behind them.

## How Companion Planting Works

Plants interact with their neighbors in several practical ways:

- **Pest deterrence** — strong-scented plants can mask the smell of vulnerable crops or actively repel specific insects.
- **Trap cropping** — some plants lure pests away from more valuable crops.
- **Nutrient sharing** — nitrogen-fixing plants like legumes can benefit nearby heavy feeders.
- **Physical support** — tall plants can provide structure for climbing plants.
- **Efficient space use** — pairing fast-growing and slow-growing plants maximizes garden space.

## Classic Companion Planting Combinations

### Tomatoes and Basil

Basil is thought to repel pests like aphids and whiteflies while potentially improving tomato flavor. It's also simply convenient — both are used together frequently in the kitchen.

### Carrots and Onions

Onions help mask the scent that attracts carrot flies, while carrots don't compete significantly with onions for nutrients or space, making this a highly efficient pairing.

### Corn, Beans, and Squash (The Three Sisters)

This traditional Indigenous planting method pairs corn as a natural climbing structure for beans, beans fixing nitrogen in the soil for the heavy-feeding corn, and squash spreading along the ground to suppress weeds and retain soil moisture.

### Marigolds and Almost Everything

Marigolds release compounds that deter nematodes and some soil-borne pests, making them a near-universal companion plant. They're commonly planted around the borders of vegetable beds.

### Cucumbers and Nasturtiums

Nasturtiums act as a trap crop for aphids, drawing them away from cucumbers, while also attracting pollinators that benefit cucumber fruit set.

### Lettuce and Tall Plants

Lettuce prefers some afternoon shade in warmer climates, making it a good candidate to plant beneath taller crops like corn or trellised beans.

## Plants That Should Be Kept Apart

Not every pairing is beneficial — some combinations actively work against each other:

- **Tomatoes and Brassicas (cabbage, broccoli):** Compete heavily for nutrients and can stunt each other's growth.
- **Onions and Beans:** Onions can inhibit the growth of beans and peas.
- **Fennel and Most Vegetables:** Fennel releases compounds that inhibit the growth of many nearby plants and is generally best grown in isolation.
- **Potatoes and Tomatoes:** Both are nightshades and share susceptibility to the same pests and diseases, increasing risk when planted close together.

## Building Your Own Companion Planting Plan

### Start with Your Main Crops

Identify what you most want to grow well, then research companions that specifically support those crops rather than trying to optimize every possible pairing.

### Group by Growing Needs

Even without a specific companion benefit, grouping plants with similar water, sun, and soil needs makes garden maintenance significantly easier.

### Leave Room for Beneficial Insects

Include flowering herbs like dill, yarrow, and calendula throughout your garden, not just paired with specific crops. These attract pollinators and beneficial predatory insects that benefit the entire garden.

### Keep a Garden Journal

Track what combinations worked well and which didn't over multiple seasons. Companion planting research provides a starting point, but your specific climate, soil, and pest pressures will influence real-world results.

## A Word of Caution

Companion planting isn't a substitute for good fundamental gardening practices — proper soil health, adequate watering, and pest monitoring still matter most. Think of companion planting as a helpful layer on top of solid basics, not a replacement for them.

## Start Small and Watch What Happens

You don't need to memorize a whole companion planting chart before you plant anything. Try tomatoes and basil, or the Three Sisters if you've got the space, and pay attention to what actually happens in your garden. That's more useful than any chart, since your soil and climate are their own thing.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Does companion planting really work?**
Some combinations have solid research behind them, particularly around pest deterrence and nitrogen fixing, while other claimed benefits are based more on traditional practice than scientific study.

**Can I companion plant in raised beds or containers?**
Yes — companion planting principles apply at any scale, though spacing needs to be adjusted for the smaller footprint of raised beds and containers.

**What's the easiest companion planting combination to start with?**
Tomatoes and basil is a reliable, low-risk pairing that's easy to manage even for beginner gardeners.
